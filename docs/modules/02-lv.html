Module 2 — Left Ventricular Function

Systolic dysfunction, cardiogenic shock, and regional wall motion abnormalities

1. Clinical question

What clinical question does this exam answer, and how do findings change management?

Note

The bedside decision frame: Two questions in order.

  1. Is global LV systolic function normal, reduced, or hyperdynamic? A visual estimate of ejection fraction — severely reduced, mildly-to-moderately reduced, normal, or hyperdynamic — is sufficient for bedside decision-making. You do not need a number.
  2. Are there regional wall motion abnormalities? Segmental dysfunction in a coronary distribution suggests ACS as the etiology. Global dysfunction is more consistent with cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, or sepsis-mediated depression. The distinction changes management immediately.

Bedside LV assessment answers the most fundamental question in hemodynamic resuscitation: is the pump failing? A hypotensive patient with a hyperdynamic LV does not need inotropes — they need volume, vasopressors, or treatment of the underlying distributive state. A hypotensive patient with a severely reduced EF and dilated LV is in cardiogenic shock and needs a fundamentally different approach. Getting this wrong early leads to fluid overload in cardiogenic shock or undertreated distributive shock.

2. Image acquisition

Probe, windows, and technique for this module.

Use a phased array (cardiac) probe on the cardiac preset. LV function is assessed across multiple windows — no single view is sufficient. The goal is to visualize the LV in two orthogonal planes and assess wall motion in all segments.

Parasternal long axis

Tip

What you are looking for: Global LV contractility (do the walls move vigorously inward in systole?), chamber size (a dilated LV suggests chronicity), and mitral valve excursion (reduced in low-output states). M-mode at the tips of the mitral valve leaflets allows measurement of EPSS (E-point septal separation) — a distance greater than 7 mm suggests reduced EF; greater than 13 mm correlates with severely reduced EF.

Tip

Parasternal short axis — papillary muscle level

Tip

What you are looking for: The LV should appear round in cross-section and contract concentrically and symmetrically. This is the best single view for a visual EF estimate — watch the endocardium sweep inward in systole. Also assess the interventricular septum: a flat or D-shaped LV indicates RV pressure or volume overload (see Module 3).

Apical four-chamber

Tip

What you are looking for: All four chambers, with attention to LV wall motion in each visible segment. The apical view is essential for identifying apical wall motion abnormalities (LAD territory) and for assessing LV size relative to the RV.

Subcostal

Tip

When to use: First-line in the unstable patient who cannot be repositioned. Provides a rapid visual impression of global LV function. Not sufficient alone for wall motion analysis.

3. Visual global assessment

Train your eye by comparing the spectrum of LV function side by side. Use the normal clips as your reference, then scroll through progressively reduced function.

Normal LV function (EF 55–70%)

Tip

This is your reference. The endocardial walls move briskly inward in systole and the LV cavity nearly obliterates at peak contraction. The posterior wall and septum thicken visibly. A normal study looks vigorous — if you are unsure whether the function is normal, it probably is.

Hyperdynamic LV (EF >70%)

Tip

Near-complete cavity obliteration in systole. Common in sepsis (early), anemia, hyperthyroidism, and hypovolemia. In a hypotensive patient, a hyperdynamic LV points away from cardiogenic shock — consider distributive or hypovolemic etiologies, though mixed shock states are possible.

Mildly to moderately reduced LV (EF 30–54%)

Tip

Wall motion is reduced but clearly present. The LV may or may not be dilated. Does not automatically mean cardiogenic shock — assess for hemodynamic compromise and end-organ perfusion. Common in known heart failure presenting with decompensation. Treat the precipitant: arrhythmia, ischemia, infection, medication non-compliance. Avoid negative inotropes and large fluid boluses.

Severely reduced LV (EF <30%)

Tip

Wall motion is globally reduced with minimal inward thickening. The LV is often dilated. The mitral valve opens sluggishly and EPSS is markedly increased. This is cardiogenic shock physiology until proven otherwise in a hypotensive patient. Causes include ischemic cardiomyopathy, dilated cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, Takotsubo, and sepsis-mediated depression. Avoid aggressive fluid loading. An inotrope and early consideration of mechanical circulatory support are the interventions.

4. Regional wall motion abnormalities

Regional myocardial function is assessed by wall thickening and endocardial motion. Segmental dysfunction confined to a coronary territory suggests acute coronary syndrome — the pattern tells you which vessel is involved.

Coronary perfusion territories

The LV is divided into segments that correspond to the three major coronary arteries. On the parasternal short axis at mid-papillary level:

  • LAD (left anterior descending) — anterior wall, anteroseptal wall, apex
  • RCA (right coronary artery) — inferior wall, inferoseptal wall
  • LCx (left circumflex) — lateral wall, inferolateral wall, anterolateral wall

Recognizing which segments are affected tells you which vessel is the culprit — and whether the RV is at risk (RCA territory).

Wall motion scoring

Each segment is graded by wall thickening in systole compared to diastole:

Grade Motion Wall thickening
Normal Normal endocardial inward motion >40% increase
Hypokinetic Reduced inward motion 10–40% increase
Akinetic Absent motion <10% increase
Dyskinetic Paradoxical outward motion in systole Thinning or no thickening

For focused cardiac ultrasound, the goal is to identify major abnormalities — severely hypokinetic, akinetic, or dyskinetic segments — not to formally score every segment.

Anterior wall (LAD territory)

Tip

Anterior and anteroseptal hypokinesis or akinesis on PSAX. On A4C, the apical and septal segments are affected. LAD occlusion produces the largest territory of dysfunction and is the most common cause of cardiogenic shock from acute MI.

Inferior wall (RCA territory)

Tip

Inferior and inferoseptal hypokinesis on PSAX. Always assess the RV when inferior RWMA is present — the RCA supplies the RV free wall, and an RCA occlusion can cause concomitant RV infarct. RV infarct requires volume loading and avoidance of nitrates, making it a distinct management scenario.

Lateral wall (LCx territory)

Tip

Anterolateral and inferolateral wall dysfunction on PSAX. On A4C, the lateral wall segments are affected. LCx territory infarcts are often ECG-silent (no ST elevation in standard leads) — bedside echo may be the first clue.

Septal dyskinesis

Tip

Paradoxical septal motion — the septum moves away from the LV cavity in systole instead of toward it. Can be seen after cardiac surgery (post-pericardiotomy), with a left bundle branch block, or with RV pressure overload causing septal shift. Not always ischemic — correlate with the clinical context.

Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy

Tip

Apical and mid-ventricular akinesis or hypokinesis with preserved or hyperdynamic basal segments. Does not follow a single coronary territory — this is the key distinguishing feature from LAD occlusion, which also causes apical dysfunction but includes basal anterior involvement along the LAD distribution. History of emotional or physiologic stressor. Can present with cardiogenic shock; usually recovers within days to weeks.

5. Integration cases

Cases where the finding changes management.

Case 1: Hypotension after STEMI

Clinical scenario: A 61-year-old man with an anterior STEMI is brought to the ED. He received aspirin and heparin in the field. On arrival his BP is 78/50, HR 110, and he is cool and diaphoretic. The cath lab team is being activated.

What exam would you perform? Rapid focused cardiac ultrasound before transfer to the cath lab.

Tip

What you see: Severely reduced EF. Anterior and apical wall akinesis. LV not markedly dilated (acute presentation). No pericardial effusion.

How does this change management? This is cardiogenic shock from a large anterior MI. Start an inotrope as a bridge. Avoid aggressive fluid boluses. Notify the cath lab of cardiogenic shock — they may want an IABP or Impella placed at the time of PCI. Do not delay transfer for a prolonged echo.

Case 2: Undifferentiated hypotension in the ICU

Clinical scenario: A 58-year-old woman with septic shock from a urinary source has been on norepinephrine for 18 hours. She received 4 L of crystalloid. Her BP is now 72/40 despite escalating vasopressors. Lactate is rising.

What exam would you perform? Bedside cardiac ultrasound — LV function, RV size, IVC.

Tip

What you see: Severely reduced EF. Global hypokinesis. LV not dilated. IVC is plethoric.

How does this change management? This is sepsis-mediated cardiomyopathy with cardiogenic component to the shock. Add an inotrope. Stop or slow fluid administration. Consider PA catheter or advanced hemodynamic monitoring. Avoid further escalation of vasopressors alone — you are vasoconstricting a failing heart. Reassess in 4–6 hours; sepsis-mediated depression often recovers with treatment of the underlying infection.

Case 3: Chest pain and new ECG changes

Clinical scenario: A 49-year-old woman presents with 2 hours of substernal chest pressure and diaphoresis. ECG shows ST depression in V4–V6. She is hemodynamically stable.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound to assess for RWMA.

Tip

What you see: Lateral wall hypokinesis. Preserved global function. No effusion.

How does this change management? Lateral RWMA in the context of ST depression and chest pain is an NSTEMI with high-risk features. Activate the cath lab urgently. Anticoagulate. The echo finding corroborates the clinical diagnosis and raises urgency — do not wait for troponin results.

Case 4: Young patient with viral prodrome and shock

Clinical scenario: A 28-year-old man presents with 5 days of viral URI symptoms followed by 24 hours of progressive dyspnea and now hypotension (BP 88/60). He has no cardiac history.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound.

Tip

What you see: Severely reduced EF. Globally hypokinetic but not dilated LV. No RWMA pattern. Small pericardial effusion.

How does this change management? Myocarditis with cardiogenic shock. This is not ischemic cardiomyopathy — no regional pattern, young patient, viral prodrome. Start an inotrope. Urgent cardiology consultation for consideration of mechanical circulatory support — myocarditis-related shock can be rapidly fatal and some patients benefit from short-term MCS as a bridge to recovery. MRI is the gold standard for diagnosis but should not delay resuscitation.

Tip

Case format reminder: The case ends with a management decision, not just a diagnosis.

Quiz

Note

Q1. A hypotensive patient has a hyperdynamic LV on bedside echo. What does this tell you about the etiology of shock?

Caution

Answer Hyperdynamic LV function argues against cardiogenic shock as the primary etiology. Consider distributive shock (sepsis, anaphylaxis), hypovolemia, or obstructive shock. Mixed shock states are possible — a hyperdynamic LV does not guarantee adequate cardiac output if heart rate is severely elevated.

Q2. What is EPSS, and what does an elevated value indicate?

Caution

Answer E-point septal separation — the distance between the anterior mitral leaflet at its maximum opening excursion (E-point) and the interventricular septum, measured by M-mode on PLAX. Normal is less than 7 mm. Values above 7 mm correlate with reduced EF. It is a useful single-number surrogate when visual estimation is uncertain.

Q3. You see hypokinesis of the inferior wall and posterior wall on PSAX. What coronary territory does this implicate, and what additional structure should you assess?

Caution

Answer Right coronary artery (RCA) territory. Always assess the RV when inferior RWMA is present — the RCA supplies the RV free wall and an RCA occlusion can cause concomitant RV infarct. RV infarct requires volume loading and avoidance of nitrates, making it a distinct management scenario.

Q4. A patient has a severely reduced EF with a markedly dilated LV. A second patient has a severely reduced EF with a normal-sized LV. What does the difference in LV size suggest?

Caution

Answer Dilation suggests a chronic process — the heart has had time to remodel (dilated cardiomyopathy, longstanding ischemic disease). A non-dilated LV with severely reduced EF suggests an acute process: myocarditis, Takotsubo, acute large MI, or acute decompensation of previously compensated disease.

Q5. What is the Takotsubo pattern, and how does it differ from an LAD occlusion on bedside echo?

Caution

Answer Takotsubo (apical ballooning syndrome) shows apical and mid-ventricular hypokinesis or akinesis with preserved or hyperdynamic basal segments. An LAD occlusion causes anterior and apical dysfunction but typically includes some basal anterior involvement and follows the LAD distribution more closely. Takotsubo does not respect a single coronary territory — the basal sparing is the key distinguishing feature.
