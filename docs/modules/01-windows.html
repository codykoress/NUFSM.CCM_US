Module 1 — Windows & Acquisition

Effusion, tamponade, and pericardial pathology at the bedside

1. Clinical question

What clinical question does this exam answer, and how do findings change management?

Note

The bedside decision frame: Three questions in order.

  1. Is there a pericardial effusion? An echo-free space surrounding the heart — confirmed in at least two views before acting on it.
  2. Is it physiologically significant? Size alone does not determine significance. Look for chamber collapse, plethoric IVC, and respiratory variation — the hallmarks of elevated intrapericardial pressure.
  3. Does this change what you do right now? Tamponade is a clinical and sonographic diagnosis. A large effusion in a hemodynamically stable patient may warrant monitoring and urgent cardiology consultation. The same effusion in a hypotensive patient with RA collapse is a needle emergency.

Pericardial ultrasound answers a binary, time-sensitive question at the bedside: does this patient need pericardiocentesis now? It also identifies effusions that will influence resuscitation strategy — for example, recognizing that aggressive fluid loading in tamponade provides only a brief bridge, and that vasopressors are a temporizing measure, not a solution. Getting the exam right means getting to the right intervention faster.

2. Image acquisition

Probe, windows, and technique for this module.

Use a phased array (cardiac) probe for all windows. The footprint is small enough to fit between ribs, and the sector format gives the wide near-field view needed to see the full pericardium. If only a curvilinear probe is available, it is an acceptable substitute; a linear probe is not.

Set the machine to the cardiac preset — this inverts the color map convention and optimizes depth and gain for cardiac structures. Adjust depth so that the posterior pericardium is visible with a small margin beyond it; a common error is insufficient depth that clips the posterior effusion.

Subcostal four-chamber view

Tip

Video slot: Subcostal four-chamber acquisition — probe position, indicator orientation, fanning technique.

Probe placement: Subxiphoid, probe nearly flat against the abdomen (indicator to patient’s left, probe angle 15–20° above horizontal). Use the left lobe of the liver as an acoustic window.

How to optimize: Ask the patient to take a slow breath in and hold — this drops the diaphragm and brings the heart closer to the probe. Apply firm, steady pressure rather than jabbing. If the view is poor, try a more lateral subxiphoid position or ask the patient to bend their knees.

What you are looking for: All four chambers visible simultaneously, with the right ventricle nearest the probe. The pericardium appears as a bright echogenic line surrounding the heart. An effusion appears as an anechoic (black) stripe between the visceral and parietal pericardium.

Why this window first: In undifferentiated shock or cardiac arrest, the subcostal view is fastest to obtain and does not require repositioning. It is the window most likely to be achievable in a supine, ventilated patient.

Parasternal long axis

Tip

Video slot: Parasternal long axis acquisition — rib interspace selection, indicator to right shoulder, optimizing the view.

Probe placement: Left parasternal border, 3rd or 4th intercostal space, indicator pointing toward the patient’s right shoulder (roughly 10–11 o’clock). The left ventricle, mitral valve, aortic valve, left atrium, and proximal descending thoracic aorta are all visible in the same plane.

How to optimize: Move up or down one rib space if the view is cut off. Tilting the probe slightly lateral or medial adjusts the imaging plane through the long axis of the LV. The aortic valve should open symmetrically and the LV walls should appear equal in thickness.

What you are looking for: An effusion in PLAX appears as an echo-free stripe posterior to the LV, between the posterior LV wall and the pericardium. Key landmark: the descending thoracic aorta appears in cross-section at the far left of the image. Fluid anterior to the aorta is pericardial. Fluid posterior to the aorta is pleural. This distinction matters.

Apical four-chamber

Tip

Video slot: Apical four-chamber acquisition — point of maximal impulse, lateral decubitus positioning, depth and gain adjustments.

Probe placement: Point of maximal impulse (PMI), typically 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line. Indicator points to the patient’s left (3 o’clock). In obese patients or those with COPD, the PMI may be displaced; palpate for it rather than assuming position.

How to optimize: Left lateral decubitus positioning brings the heart toward the chest wall and dramatically improves image quality. Reduce depth so the apex is at the top of the screen (in cardiac convention, apex-up). Apical windows are often the most difficult — if the view is poor, try moving one interspace lateral or inferior.

What you are looking for: All four chambers, with the apex at top and the AV valves in the center. Effusion appears as echo-free space surrounding the heart. The apical view is particularly useful for identifying loculated effusions and for assessing respiratory variation across the mitral and tricuspid valves when tamponade physiology is suspected.

3. Normal & normal variants

The library of normal hearts and common confounders.

Fellows need to see ten or more normal studies before pathology is interpretable. The most common error is calling a normal structure an effusion — or missing a small effusion because the normal pericardium looks similar. Below are the key normals and variants to recognize.

Tip

Video slots: Normal studies across body habitus — lean, obese, COPD, post-CABG. Review each before proceeding to pathology.

Normal pericardium

The normal pericardium is a thin, bright (hyperechoic) line that moves with the heart. There is no echo-free space between the visceral and parietal layers in a normal study. A trace of fluid (up to 50 mL physiologically) may appear as a very thin stripe posteriorly on PLAX — this is normal and does not warrant intervention.

Pericardial fat pad

The most common mimic. Pericardial fat is echogenic (not echo-free), most prominent anteriorly, and does not shift with patient positioning. On subcostal view it appears as a bright, heterogeneous stripe anterior to the RV. Key distinguishing features:

  • Fat is echogenic; effusion is anechoic.
  • Fat does not change with the cardiac cycle; effusion may show swirling or layering.
  • Fat is anterior; hemodynamically significant effusions tend to be circumferential and posterior.

Epicardial fat

Epicardial fat sits between the myocardium and the visceral pericardium. It is visible as a thin echogenic layer, most prominent over the RV free wall and in the AV groove. It moves with the heart (unlike pericardial fat pad, which is relatively fixed). Distinguishing epicardial fat from a small effusion requires attention to echogenicity and motion.

Pleural effusion

A left pleural effusion appears posterior to the descending aorta on PLAX. Pericardial effusion appears anterior to the aorta. This landmark — the descending thoracic aorta — is the single most important landmark for distinguishing the two. Large left pleural effusions can also compress the heart and produce hemodynamic findings that mimic tamponade (so-called “pleural tamponade”).

Post-CABG appearance

After cardiac surgery, the pericardium is often left open or only partially closed. This creates an atypical pericardial appearance with adhesions, thickening, and potential for loculated collections. Post-operative effusions are frequently anterior and loculated — they may not be visible on subcostal view alone. The clinical context (recent sternotomy, new hemodynamic instability) should lower the threshold for a comprehensive multi-view exam or formal echo.

4. Pathology library

Organized by finding, not by diagnosis.

Small effusion

Tip

Video slot: Small posterior effusion on PLAX — thin echo-free stripe, preserved chamber function.

A small effusion is generally defined as less than 1 cm in greatest diameter on PLAX, posterior to the LV. It is visible in diastole and may disappear or thin in systole.

Teaching points:

  • Visible posteriorly first — the most dependent space when supine.
  • Does not produce chamber collapse.
  • Causes: pericarditis, viral illness, malignancy, uremia, post-procedure.
  • A small effusion in the right clinical context (chest pain, pleuritic quality, friction rub) supports the diagnosis of acute pericarditis and warrants NSAIDs and follow-up imaging, not pericardiocentesis.

Moderate to large effusion

Tip

Video slot: Large circumferential effusion — swinging heart, echo-free space in all windows.

Moderate: 1–2 cm; large: greater than 2 cm on PLAX. Large effusions are typically circumferential and visible in all windows.

Teaching points:

  • The “swinging heart” — pendular motion of the heart within a large effusion — is a classic finding and a marker of significant hemodynamic risk.
  • Size does not equal tamponade. A large chronic effusion (e.g., malignant) may be hemodynamically tolerated because the pericardium has had time to stretch. An acute effusion of even 200 mL (e.g., post-procedure, trauma) can cause tamponade because the pericardium has not had time to accommodate.
  • Always assess chamber collapse and IVC when a large effusion is present.

Tamponade physiology

Tip

Video slots:

  • RA free wall collapse in systole (subcostal or apical)
  • RV diastolic collapse (PLAX or subcostal)
  • Plethoric, non-collapsing IVC
  • Respiratory variation on mitral inflow (M-mode or PW Doppler)

Tamponade is elevated intrapericardial pressure exceeding intracardiac filling pressure. It is a clinical diagnosis confirmed sonographically. No single finding is pathognomonic — the diagnosis is integrative.

Sonographic findings, in order of sensitivity:

Finding View Notes
RA free wall collapse (systole) Subcostal, apical Earliest sign; sensitive but not specific
RV diastolic collapse PLAX, subcostal More specific; correlates with pressure equalization
Plethoric IVC (>2.1 cm, <50% collapse) Subcostal IVC view Reflects elevated right-sided filling pressures
Respiratory variation on mitral inflow >25% Apical, PW Doppler Sonographic equivalent of pulsus paradoxus

Management implication: When tamponade physiology is present in a hemodynamically unstable patient, the exam is over. Call for pericardiocentesis or emergent cardiothoracic surgery depending on the clinical context (medical vs. traumatic/surgical). Do not delay for formal echo.

Loculated effusion

Tip

Video slot: Post-operative loculated effusion — anterior, irregular, compressing RV.

Loculated effusions do not distribute freely around the heart. They occur after cardiac surgery, trauma, or prior pericarditis with adhesions. The collection may compress only one chamber, producing atypical hemodynamic presentations that do not fit classic tamponade physiology.

Teaching points:

  • Subcostal view may be normal; always check all windows.
  • Echogenic or heterogeneous fluid suggests blood, clot, or exudate.
  • Requires formal echo and cardiac surgery consultation — bedside pericardiocentesis is often not safe or effective for loculated collections.

5. Integration cases

Cases where the finding changes management.

Case 1: Post-procedure hypotension

Clinical scenario: A 68-year-old man undergoes a right heart catheterization for pulmonary hypertension workup. Thirty minutes after the procedure he is hypotensive (BP 82/50), tachycardic (HR 118), and mildly diaphoretic. He denies chest pain. The team is considering IV fluids and a vasopressor.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound, subcostal view first.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Large circumferential effusion. RA free wall collapses in systole. RV diastolic collapse present on subcostal and PLAX. IVC is 2.4 cm and non-collapsing.

How does this change management? Stop fluids — they will not correct tamponade. Call interventional cardiology and cardiac surgery simultaneously. Prepare for emergent pericardiocentesis. Do not start a vasopressor as the primary intervention; it temporizes without fixing the problem.

Case 2: Dyspnea in a cancer patient

Clinical scenario: A 54-year-old woman with metastatic lung adenocarcinoma presents with two weeks of worsening exertional dyspnea and a new feeling of fullness in her chest. BP 104/72, HR 102, RR 20. JVD is present. Chest X-ray shows an enlarged cardiac silhouette.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound — subcostal and PLAX minimum.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Very large circumferential effusion with swinging heart motion. No chamber collapse. IVC is 2.2 cm with minimal respiratory variation.

How does this change management? This patient is not in tamponade now but is at high risk. Oncology and cardiology consultation for semi-elective pericardiocentesis or pericardial window. Admit for monitoring. Do not discharge based on the absence of tamponade physiology at this moment — large malignant effusions can decompensate rapidly.

Case 3: Undifferentiated shock in the ED

Clinical scenario: A 44-year-old man arrives by EMS after being found unresponsive at home. BP 70/40, HR 124, GCS 10. No history available. He is cool, mottled, and has distended neck veins.

What exam would you perform? Immediate subcostal cardiac view as part of the resuscitation.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Moderate effusion. RA collapse in systole. No obvious RV collapse, but image quality is limited.

How does this change management? Activate the pericardiocentesis team. Limit IV fluids to one bolus as a bridge. Avoid deep sedation if intubation is needed — it can precipitate cardiac arrest in tamponade by eliminating sympathetic tone. If the patient arrests, perform pericardiocentesis before standard ACLS measures; defibrillation will not work if the heart cannot fill.

Case 4: Dialysis patient with chest pain

Clinical scenario: A 62-year-old man on hemodialysis three times weekly presents with sharp chest pain worse lying flat and better leaning forward. He missed his last two dialysis sessions. Vitals are stable. ECG shows diffuse ST elevation with PR depression.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound to assess for effusion and tamponade physiology.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Small-to-moderate posterior effusion. No chamber collapse. IVC is 2.5 cm (consistent with volume overload from missed dialysis).

How does this change management? Arrange urgent dialysis — this is the treatment for uremic pericarditis. NSAIDs are relatively contraindicated in ESRD. Admit for monitoring and repeat ultrasound after dialysis. Serial exams are appropriate — uremic effusions can enlarge rapidly if dialysis is delayed.

Case 5: Trauma — thoracic injury

Clinical scenario: A 29-year-old woman is brought in after a high-speed motor vehicle collision. She is intubated in the field. BP 88/60 on two vasopressors, HR 135. Bilateral breath sounds. No obvious external thoracic trauma.

What exam would you perform? FAST exam with focused cardiac view as the first priority.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Echo-free stripe anterior to the RV on subcostal view. RV diastolic collapse present. No obvious pleural effusions.

How does this change management? Emergent cardiothoracic surgery consultation. In a patient this unstable, pericardiocentesis alone is a bridge — the underlying injury requires operative repair. If the patient arrests, emergency department thoracotomy with pericardiotomy is indicated per ATLS protocol. Do not spend time on CT imaging.

Tip

Case format reminder: The case ends with a management decision, not just a diagnosis. The exam is a tool for action.

Note

Q1. On parasternal long axis, you see an echo-free space posterior to the LV. How do you determine whether it is pericardial or pleural?

Caution

Answer Locate the descending thoracic aorta in cross-section at the far left of the PLAX image. Fluid anterior to the aorta is pericardial. Fluid posterior to the aorta is pleural.

Q2. A patient has a large effusion and is hypotensive. You look for RV diastolic collapse but image quality is poor. What other findings support tamponade physiology?

Caution

Answer RA free wall collapse in systole (more sensitive, less specific), plethoric non-collapsing IVC (>2.1 cm, <50% inspiratory collapse), and respiratory variation on mitral inflow >25% by PW Doppler. No single finding is required — the diagnosis is integrative.

Q3. You see an echogenic structure anterior to the RV that does not move with the cardiac cycle. What is the most likely diagnosis, and how do you confirm it?

Caution

Answer Pericardial fat pad. Confirm by noting echogenicity (not echo-free), anterior location, and absence of positional shift. Reposition the patient — a true effusion will layer to the dependent position; fat will not.

Q4. A patient with known metastatic cancer has a large effusion but no chamber collapse. Is this patient safe for discharge?

Caution

Answer No. Absence of tamponade physiology at this moment does not mean it is safe to discharge. Large malignant effusions can decompensate rapidly. This patient needs admission, oncology and cardiology consultation, and a plan for drainage or a pericardial window.

Q5. In a trauma patient with tamponade physiology, why is pericardiocentesis alone often insufficient?

Caution

Answer Traumatic hemopericardium usually indicates a cardiac or great vessel injury. Pericardiocentesis may temporarily relieve pressure and buy time, but the underlying structural injury requires operative repair. Definitive management is in the operating room.
