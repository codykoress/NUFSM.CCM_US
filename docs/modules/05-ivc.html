Module 5 — IVC & Volume Status

Preload assessment, fluid responsiveness, and venous congestion

1. Clinical question

What clinical question does this exam answer, and how do findings change management?

Note

The bedside decision frame: Two questions in order.

  1. Is this patient likely to respond to a fluid bolus? A collapsible, small IVC in a spontaneously breathing patient suggests low right-sided filling pressure and likely fluid responsiveness. A plethoric, non-collapsing IVC suggests elevated venous pressure — giving fluid will not help and may cause harm.
  2. Is there venous congestion? An elevated IVC diameter with loss of respiratory variation, combined with hepatic vein dilation and portal vein pulsatility, indicates systemic venous hypertension. This pattern shifts the frame from “give fluid” to “treat the cause of congestion.”

The IVC is the most accessible venous structure for bedside hemodynamic assessment. It is also the most misused. A collapsible IVC does not guarantee that a fluid bolus will improve cardiac output — it indicates that the right atrial pressure is low, which is one of several conditions necessary for fluid responsiveness. Equally important, a plethoric IVC is not always tamponade — it can reflect right heart failure, PE, severe tricuspid regurgitation, or aggressive fluid resuscitation. The IVC is one data point in a hemodynamic assessment, not a standalone decision-maker.

2. Image acquisition

Probe, windows, and technique for this module.

Use a phased array or curvilinear probe from the subcostal window. The IVC enters the right atrium from below. The image plane is optimized to show the IVC in its long axis as it traverses the liver toward the RA.

Subcostal IVC view

Tip

IVC acquisition — probe position, long axis of the IVC, measurement technique, respiratory variation.

Probe placement: Subcostal, indicator to the patient’s right (or toward the head depending on your convention), angled to bring the IVC into view as it enters the RA. The hepatic vein confluence should be visible entering the IVC.

Measurement: IVC diameter is measured 1.5–2.0 cm distal to the RA–IVC junction (typically just proximal to the hepatic vein confluence), per ASE convention. Measure at end-expiration for the resting diameter, then assess collapse with a sniff (spontaneous breathing) or the distensibility index (mechanical ventilation).

Common error: Imaging the aorta instead of the IVC. The IVC is to the patient’s right of midline, collapses with inspiration, and enters the RA. The aorta is to the left, pulsatile, and does not collapse.

Hepatic vein assessment

Tip

Hepatic vein PW Doppler — normal anterograde flow vs diastolic flow reversal in venous congestion.

Probe placement: Same subcostal window, angle to bring the hepatic veins into view entering the IVC. Sample volume placed in the hepatic vein 1–2 cm from the IVC junction.

What you are looking for: Normal hepatic vein flow is predominantly anterograde (systolic and diastolic waves toward the RA) with small physiologic reversals. Diastolic flow reversal prominent with expiration suggests elevated right-sided filling pressure (constrictive physiology). Systolic flow reversal suggests severe tricuspid regurgitation.

Portal vein pulsatility

Tip

Portal vein PW Doppler — normally monophasic flow vs pulsatile flow in severe venous congestion.

What you are looking for: The portal vein normally has continuous, mildly pulsatile anterograde flow. When systemic venous pressure is severely elevated, this flow becomes markedly pulsatile or even reverses — a sign of severe venous congestion transmitted to the portal system. Portal vein pulsatility fraction >50% is associated with worse outcomes in ICU patients and in those with right heart failure.

3. Normal & normal variants

Tip

Normal IVC — diameter 1.5–2.1 cm, >50% collapsibility with sniff. IVC in an athlete (mildly enlarged but still collapsible).

Normal IVC

Diameter 1.5–2.1 cm at end-expiration, with greater than 50% collapsibility with a maximal sniff or inspiration. This corresponds to an estimated RA pressure of 0–5 mmHg.

The athlete’s IVC

Well-trained athletes may have mildly enlarged IVCs (up to 2.5 cm) at rest due to increased plasma volume and vagal tone, with preserved or even exaggerated collapsibility. Clinical context is essential — a large IVC in a healthy, euvolemic runner is not the same as a large IVC in a patient with right heart failure.

IVC in mechanically ventilated patients

Respiratory variation is reversed in mechanically ventilated patients — the IVC distends with positive pressure insufflation (during inspiration) and collapses with expiration. Use the distensibility index rather than collapsibility: (IVC max − IVC min) / IVC min × 100%. A distensibility index greater than 18% predicts fluid responsiveness in passively ventilated patients without spontaneous breathing efforts. This rule does not apply if the patient is triggering the ventilator, has high PEEP, or has low tidal volumes (<8 mL/kg).

4. Pathology library

Organized by finding, not by diagnosis.

Small, collapsible IVC

Tip

Small IVC — diameter <1.5 cm, >50% collapse, “flat” appearance.

Diameter less than 1.5 cm with greater than 50% respiratory collapse. Estimated RA pressure 0–5 mmHg.

Teaching points:

  • Consistent with hypovolemia, distributive shock, or effective diuresis.
  • In a hypotensive patient, a small collapsible IVC suggests volume responsiveness and supports a cautious fluid trial.
  • Does not guarantee that fluid will improve cardiac output — also requires adequate LV and RV function to translate preload into stroke volume.
  • Serial assessment after a fluid bolus (did the IVC change? did cardiac output improve?) is more informative than a single measurement.

Plethoric, non-collapsing IVC

Tip

Plethoric IVC — diameter >2.1 cm, <50% collapse, associated with elevated RA pressure.

Diameter greater than 2.1 cm with less than 50% respiratory collapse. Estimated RA pressure 10–20 mmHg.

Teaching points:

  • Does not equal tamponade — this is one finding among several. Always assess for chamber collapse and clinical context.
  • Causes: right heart failure, massive PE, cardiac tamponade, constrictive pericarditis, severe TR, aggressive fluid resuscitation, high PEEP.
  • In this setting, additional fluid boluses are unlikely to be beneficial and may worsen venous congestion.
  • The priority is identifying and treating the cause of elevated RA pressure.

Venous congestion pattern

Tip
  • Plethoric IVC with diastolic hepatic vein flow reversal
  • Pulsatile portal vein flow

A syndrome of IVC dilation, hepatic vein abnormalities, and portal vein pulsatility reflecting severe systemic venous hypertension. Associated with worse outcomes in patients with right heart failure, cardiogenic shock, and septic shock.

Teaching points:

  • Venous congestion causes end-organ dysfunction — congested kidneys, a congested liver, and a congested gut are not simply “full,” they are physiologically impaired.
  • Recognition of venous congestion should prompt de-escalation of fluid administration and active decongestion (diuresis, ultrafiltration) even in patients who appear to need more fluid for other reasons.
  • The RVSP-VExUS framework (combining IVC, hepatic vein, and portal vein findings) grades congestion severity and has prognostic implications.

Intermediate IVC

Diameter 1.5–2.1 cm with variable collapse. Estimated RA pressure 5–10 mmHg. This is the most common and least definitive finding — the IVC alone cannot reliably predict fluid responsiveness in this range.

Teaching points:

  • Use dynamic assessments instead: passive leg raise (PLR) with cardiac output monitoring, or a mini-fluid challenge with serial stroke volume measurement.
  • A PLR that transiently increases stroke volume (or pulse pressure) by >10% predicts fluid responsiveness with high sensitivity and specificity, regardless of IVC status.
  • Do not make a high-stakes fluid decision based on an intermediate IVC in isolation.

5. Integration cases

Cases where the finding changes management.

Case 1: Septic shock — is this patient fluid responsive?

Clinical scenario: A 72-year-old woman with urosepsis has received 3 L of crystalloid and is on norepinephrine 0.2 mcg/kg/min. BP is 84/52, HR 112. Lactate is 4.1 mmol/L and trending up slightly. The team is debating another fluid bolus.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound with IVC assessment.

Tip

Loops for interpretation.

What you see: IVC 1.4 cm with >70% collapse on sniff. LV hyperdynamic. RV normal size. No effusion.

How does this change management? Small, collapsible IVC with hyperdynamic LV — consistent with distributive physiology and likely fluid responsiveness. Administer a cautious fluid bolus (250–500 mL) and reassess. If lactate clears and hemodynamics improve, the fluid was beneficial. If not, escalate vasopressor therapy rather than continuing to fluid load. The hyperdynamic LV argues against a cardiogenic component.

Case 2: The patient who won’t respond to fluids

Clinical scenario: An 80-year-old man with CHF (EF 25%) and pneumonia is admitted with hypotension. He has received 2 L of saline. BP remains 86/54. Creatinine is rising. He has bilateral crackles.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound with IVC and hepatic vein assessment.

Tip

Loops for interpretation.

What you see: IVC 2.6 cm, non-collapsing. Plethoric hepatic veins with diastolic flow reversal. Pulsatile portal vein. Severely reduced EF. Bilateral pleural effusions.

How does this change management? Stop fluids immediately — this patient is severely congested and has cardiogenic shock. The rising creatinine is from renal venous congestion, not from hypovolemia. Additional fluid will worsen pulmonary edema and organ congestion further. Initiate dobutamine for inotropic support. Cautious diuresis to reduce venous congestion. Consider early nephrology involvement if oliguria persists despite decongestion.

Case 3: Post-resuscitation — how much fluid did this patient get?

Clinical scenario: A 55-year-old man is post-arrest, now intubated and sedated. He received 4 L during the resuscitation. BP on norepinephrine is 92/60. The ICU team is reviewing hemodynamic targets.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound with IVC distensibility assessment (mechanically ventilated).

Tip

Loops for interpretation.

What you see: IVC 2.3 cm. Distensibility index 10% (below the 18% threshold). Normal LV function. RV mildly enlarged.

How does this change management? Not fluid responsive by distensibility criteria. The patient received significant resuscitation volume and the RV is mildly enlarged. Avoid additional fluid boluses. Hold further resuscitation fluid. Focus on optimizing vasopressor titration and monitoring end-organ recovery. Reassess in 4–6 hours.

Case 4: Distinguishing cardiogenic from septic shock with the IVC

Clinical scenario: A 68-year-old man with no known cardiac history is brought in with BP 76/44, HR 118, and altered mental status. Temp 38.9°C. Lactate 5.2 mmol/L. The team is debating whether to give fluid or start inotropes.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound — LV, RV, IVC.

Tip

Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Severely reduced EF. Globally hypokinetic LV, not dilated. IVC 2.4 cm, non-collapsing. No effusion. Mild RV dilation.

How does this change management? This is not septic shock with a responsive ventricle — it is cardiogenic shock (or mixed shock with a dominant cardiogenic component), likely sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy or a new ischemic event. The plethoric IVC confirms elevated filling pressures. Start dobutamine. Limit fluids to what is required for medication delivery. Obtain an ECG and troponin. Investigate the cause of the cardiomyopathy — myocarditis, ischemia, and sepsis-induced depression are all in the differential.

Tip

Case format reminder: The case ends with a management decision, not just a diagnosis.

Quiz

Note

Q1. A patient has an IVC of 1.8 cm with 40% collapse. What does this tell you about fluid responsiveness?

Caution

Answer An intermediate IVC (1.5–2.1 cm) with intermediate collapse is not reliably predictive of fluid responsiveness in either direction. Do not make a high-stakes fluid decision based on this alone. Use a dynamic test instead — passive leg raise with cardiac output monitoring, or a mini-fluid challenge (100–250 mL) with serial stroke volume or pulse pressure assessment.

Q2. How does the IVC behave differently in a mechanically ventilated patient compared to a spontaneously breathing patient?

Caution

Answer In spontaneous breathing, the IVC collapses with inspiration (negative intrathoracic pressure increases venous return). In mechanical ventilation, the IVC distends with insufflation (positive pressure reduces venous return). Use the distensibility index in ventilated patients: (IVC max − IVC min) / IVC min >18% predicts responsiveness. This only applies to passive ventilation without spontaneous efforts and at tidal volumes ≥8 mL/kg.

Q3. What is the clinical significance of a pulsatile portal vein on Doppler?

Caution

Answer Marked portal vein pulsatility (pulsatility fraction >50%) indicates severe systemic venous hypertension transmitted to the portal circulation. It is associated with end-organ congestion (renal, hepatic), worse clinical outcomes, and suggests that further fluid administration is likely harmful. It is a key component of the VExUS congestion assessment framework.

Q4. A patient in septic shock has a plethoric IVC. A colleague suggests giving another fluid bolus because “the lactate is still elevated.” How do you respond?

Caution

Answer A plethoric, non-collapsing IVC indicates elevated right atrial pressure — the patient is not preload-deficient. Giving more fluid is unlikely to improve cardiac output and may worsen venous congestion, impairing organ perfusion. Elevated lactate in this context may reflect impaired hepatic lactate clearance from congestion, not ongoing tissue hypoperfusion from inadequate preload. Focus on treating the cause of the elevated filling pressure and optimize vasopressor or inotropic support.

Q5. You image the IVC and find a large, pulsatile structure to the left of the spine. What did you image, and how do you correct it?

Caution

Answer You imaged the descending aorta, not the IVC. The aorta is to the left of midline, is pulsatile (arterial waveform on Doppler), and does not communicate with the right atrium. To correct: move slightly to the right of midline, angle toward the right atrium, and look for the structure that enters the RA from below with a venous waveform on Doppler.
