Module 4 — Pericardium

Pericarditis, constriction, and advanced pericardial assessment

1. Clinical question

What clinical question does this exam answer, and how do findings change management?

Note

The bedside decision frame: Two questions beyond the basics covered in Module 1.

  1. Is this pericarditis, and is there an effusion that requires intervention? The diagnosis of acute pericarditis is primarily clinical (pleuritic chest pain, friction rub, ECG changes), but echo defines the effusion, tracks its size, and identifies patients at highest risk of progression to tamponade.
  2. Is this constrictive pericarditis? Constriction is the great mimic of right heart failure and the one pericardial diagnosis that requires surgical intervention. It is frequently missed on bedside echo but there are clues — knowing what to look for can prompt the right specialist consultation.

This module builds on the effusion and tamponade content from Module 1. The focus here is on the clinical syndromes that follow pericardial injury — acute pericarditis, recurrent pericarditis, effusive-constrictive disease, and constrictive pericarditis — and on the more nuanced echo features that inform risk stratification and referral.

2. Image acquisition

Probe, windows, and technique for this module.

The acquisition technique for pericardial assessment is the same as covered in Module 1 (subcostal, PLAX, apical four-chamber). This module adds targeted assessment for constrictive physiology, which requires Doppler interrogation beyond standard 2D imaging.

Mitral inflow — pulsed wave Doppler

Tip

Video slot: PW Doppler at mitral valve tips — E/A ratio, respiratory variation in constriction vs normal.

Probe: Phased array, apical four-chamber. Sample volume at the tips of the mitral valve leaflets. Assess E-wave and A-wave velocity and their variation with respiration.

What you are looking for: In constrictive pericarditis, mitral E-wave velocity decreases by more than 25% with inspiration (the opposite of what happens in normal physiology). This is the Doppler equivalent of pulsus paradoxus and reflects exaggerated interventricular dependence due to the rigid pericardial shell.

Tricuspid inflow — pulsed wave Doppler

Tip

Video slot: PW Doppler at tricuspid valve tips — reciprocal respiratory variation (increases with inspiration in constriction).

What you are looking for: In constriction, as mitral E-wave falls with inspiration, tricuspid E-wave rises reciprocally. This exaggerated and reciprocal respiratory variation across the AV valves is the hallmark of constrictive physiology.

Hepatic vein flow — pulsed wave Doppler

Tip

Video slot: Hepatic vein PW Doppler — expiratory diastolic flow reversal in constriction.

What you are looking for: In constriction, diastolic flow reversal in the hepatic veins is prominent with expiration (opposite of restrictive cardiomyopathy, where reversal is with inspiration). This finding, combined with the AV valve respiratory variation, is highly specific for constrictive pericarditis.

3. Normal & normal variants

Normal pericardium on 2D

As reviewed in Module 1: thin, bright echogenic line, no echo-free space between layers, moves with the heart. A normal pericardium on 2D does not rule out constrictive pericarditis — the thickened, calcified pericardium of constriction is visible in only a subset of cases on standard echo.

Normal mitral inflow Doppler

E/A ratio 0.8–1.5 in normal adults. Mild respiratory variation (<25%) is normal. The E-wave represents early passive filling; the A-wave represents atrial contraction. Both should be present in sinus rhythm.

Normal hepatic vein flow

Predominantly anterograde systolic and diastolic flow toward the heart, with minor physiologic reversal at end-systole. Marked diastolic flow reversal is not normal.

4. Pathology library

Organized by finding, not by diagnosis.

Acute pericarditis with effusion

Tip

Video slot: Small-to-moderate effusion in the context of pericarditis — preserved function, no tamponade physiology.

Acute pericarditis is an inflammatory syndrome. Echo is used to characterize and track the effusion, not to make the diagnosis (which is clinical). Most effusions in acute pericarditis are small and do not require drainage.

Risk features that warrant closer monitoring and possible hospitalization:

  • Large effusion (>2 cm)
  • Tamponade physiology (see Module 1)
  • Fever >38°C with subacute onset
  • Immunocompromised state
  • Anticoagulation (risk of hemopericardium)
  • Failure to respond to NSAIDs within 1 week

Teaching points:

  • A normal echo does not rule out acute pericarditis.
  • Colchicine reduces recurrence and should be added to NSAIDs in all cases without contraindication.
  • Serial echo at 1 week to assess effusion trajectory is appropriate when an effusion is present at diagnosis.

Myopericarditis

Tip

Video slot: Pericarditis with regional or global LV dysfunction — the myopericarditis overlap syndrome.

When myocardial inflammation accompanies pericarditis, LV function may be impaired. This is more common than recognized and ranges from subtle wall motion abnormality to frank cardiomyopathy.

Teaching points:

  • Any patient with pericarditis and an elevated troponin should have LV function assessed by echo.
  • Regional wall motion abnormalities suggest myocardial involvement, not just pericardial.
  • Activity restriction is prolonged in myopericarditis (3–6 months vs 2–4 weeks for isolated pericarditis).
  • NSAIDs may be harmful if significant myocarditis is present — cardiology consultation is warranted.

Effusive-constrictive pericarditis

Tip

Video slot: Effusion with constrictive physiology — persistence of elevated filling pressures after pericardiocentesis.

A syndrome where both effusion and constrictive pericardial thickening coexist. The effusion may be the dominant finding initially, but after drainage, elevated filling pressures and constrictive Doppler features persist.

Teaching points:

  • Suspect this diagnosis when a patient fails to improve hemodynamically after pericardiocentesis.
  • Common causes: tuberculosis, radiation-induced pericardial disease, malignancy.
  • Requires formal echo with Doppler after drainage to identify residual constriction.
  • May ultimately require pericardiectomy.

Constrictive pericarditis

Tip

Video slots: - Septal bounce on M-mode or 2D — the abrupt early diastolic septal shift - Respiratory variation on mitral inflow (>25% E-wave decrease with inspiration) - Reciprocal tricuspid inflow increase with inspiration - Expiratory hepatic vein diastolic flow reversal

Constrictive pericarditis results from a thickened, fibrotic, or calcified pericardium that limits diastolic filling. It is the great mimic of right heart failure, liver disease, and restrictive cardiomyopathy.

Key 2D features:

  • Pericardial thickening or calcification (may not be visible on echo; CT is more sensitive)
  • Septal bounce — an abrupt early diastolic shift of the interventricular septum, reflecting exaggerated ventricular interdependence
  • Normal or mildly elevated RVSP (unlike restrictive CMP, where PA pressures are often markedly elevated)
  • Dilated IVC and hepatic veins (elevated venous pressure)

Key Doppler features:

  • Mitral E-wave decrease >25% with inspiration
  • Reciprocal tricuspid E-wave increase with inspiration
  • Expiratory diastolic flow reversal in hepatic veins

Distinguishing constriction from restriction:

Feature Constriction Restriction
Pericardium Thickened/calcified Normal
Septal bounce Present Absent
Respiratory variation Exaggerated (>25%) Minimal
Tissue Doppler e’ Normal or elevated Reduced
PA pressure Near-normal Often elevated
Hepatic vein flow reversal Expiratory Inspiratory

Teaching points:

  • Constriction is surgically curable — pericardiectomy — but carries significant operative risk. Early identification and referral to a center with expertise matters.
  • Bedside echo can raise suspicion; formal echo with comprehensive Doppler is required to confirm.
  • Diuresis can provide temporary symptomatic relief but does not treat the underlying physiology.

5. Integration cases

Cases where the finding changes management.

Case 1: Recurrent chest pain after pericarditis

Clinical scenario: A 35-year-old man had an episode of acute pericarditis 3 months ago, treated with NSAIDs. He now returns with recurrent pleuritic chest pain and low-grade fever. His prior echo showed a small effusion that had resolved at his 4-week follow-up.

What exam would you perform? Focused cardiac ultrasound to assess for recurrent effusion and LV function.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Small effusion, posterior. No tamponade physiology. Normal LV function.

How does this change management? Recurrent pericarditis with small effusion. No high-risk features. Restart NSAIDs and colchicine. If not already on colchicine, prescribe it now — it halves the recurrence rate. Cardiology consultation for refractory recurrent pericarditis (consider IL-1 inhibitor therapy such as anakinra or rilonacept if this is the second or third recurrence).

Case 2: Progressive dyspnea after radiation therapy

Clinical scenario: A 58-year-old woman with a history of mediastinal radiation for Hodgkin lymphoma 15 years ago presents with progressive exertional dyspnea, lower extremity edema, and ascites. JVD is present. Echo from her oncologist 2 years ago reportedly showed “normal function.”

What exam would you perform? Comprehensive focused cardiac ultrasound including Doppler interrogation of mitral and tricuspid inflows and hepatic veins.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Normal LV size and function. RV mildly dilated. Septal bounce visible. Plethoric IVC and hepatic veins. Mitral E-wave decreases >30% with inspiration. Expiratory diastolic flow reversal in hepatic veins.

How does this change management? Constrictive pericarditis from radiation-induced pericardial disease. This is surgically curable. Refer to cardiothoracic surgery for pericardiectomy evaluation. Formal echo and cardiac catheterization (to confirm equalization of diastolic pressures) will be required. Treat symptoms with diuresis as a bridge. This diagnosis has been present for years — the prior echo likely had sufficient Doppler data if it had been obtained.

Case 3: Post-pericardiocentesis hemodynamic instability

Clinical scenario: A 64-year-old man with tuberculosis-associated pericardial effusion underwent pericardiocentesis yesterday with drainage of 700 mL. Today he remains hypotensive (BP 86/58) and volume-overloaded, with persistent JVD and edema.

What exam would you perform? Repeat focused cardiac ultrasound with Doppler.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for interpretation.

What you see: Residual small effusion. Persistent plethoric IVC. Septal bounce. Mitral E-wave respiratory variation >25%.

How does this change management? Effusive-constrictive pericarditis — the effusion was not the only problem. Constrictive physiology persists after drainage, consistent with TB-related pericardial fibrosis. Cardiothoracic surgery consultation for pericardiectomy evaluation. Continue anti-tubercular therapy. The diagnosis of effusive-constrictive disease changes the expectation: this patient will not normalize with drainage alone.

Tip

Case format reminder: The case ends with a management decision, not just a diagnosis.

Note

Q1. What are the high-risk features in acute pericarditis that warrant hospitalization?

Caution

Answer Large effusion (>2 cm), tamponade physiology, fever >38°C with subacute onset, immunocompromised state, anticoagulation therapy, failure to respond to NSAIDs within 1 week, and evidence of myocardial involvement (elevated troponin, RWMA).

Q2. What is the septal bounce, and what does it indicate?

Caution

Answer The septal bounce (also called the septal “shudder” or diastolic septal shift) is an abrupt early diastolic shift of the interventricular septum visible on 2D echo or M-mode. It reflects exaggerated ventricular interdependence due to the rigid pericardial shell — as the ventricles compete for a fixed intrapericardial volume, septal position shifts abruptly with respiration and filling. It is a key 2D sign of constrictive pericarditis.

Q3. How does the respiratory variation pattern on mitral inflow differ between constrictive pericarditis and cardiac tamponade?

Caution

Answer Both show exaggerated respiratory variation on mitral inflow (>25% E-wave decrease with inspiration). The distinction: in tamponade, the effusion is visible and chamber collapse is the primary finding. In constriction, there may be little or no effusion — the finding is the Doppler pattern and septal bounce, not the fluid. Constrictive physiology is a diagnosis of the pericardium’s mechanical behavior, not its fluid content.

Q4. A patient has features of right heart failure (JVD, edema, ascites, elevated JVP) but normal LV function and only modestly elevated PA pressure. What diagnosis should be high on the differential?

Caution

Answer Constrictive pericarditis. The combination of elevated venous pressure, normal LV function, and near-normal PA pressure is classic for constriction — in restrictive cardiomyopathy, PA pressures are typically more elevated. Other clues: history of prior pericarditis, cardiac surgery, radiation, or TB. A septal bounce and exaggerated mitral inflow respiratory variation support the diagnosis.

Q5. A patient undergoes pericardiocentesis for a large effusion and drains 600 mL. His hemodynamics do not improve. What should you consider?

Caution

Answer Effusive-constrictive pericarditis. The effusion was not the sole cause of hemodynamic compromise — constrictive physiology from underlying pericardial thickening or fibrosis persists after drainage. Repeat Doppler assessment post-drainage will show persistent elevated filling pressures and respiratory variation. This is common in TB-related and radiation-related pericardial disease and ultimately may require pericardiectomy.
