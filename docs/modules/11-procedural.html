Module 11 — Procedural Guidance

Central venous access, thoracentesis, paracentesis, and pericardiocentesis

1. Clinical question

What clinical question does this exam answer, and how do findings change management?

Note

The bedside decision frame: One overarching principle.

Is this procedure safe to perform here, and how do I do it with the least risk to this patient?

Ultrasound guidance reduces complication rates for all four procedures covered in this module. For central venous access, real-time ultrasound has replaced landmark technique as the standard of care. For thoracentesis, paracentesis, and pericardiocentesis, ultrasound identifies the target, defines the safe approach, and allows real-time confirmation of needle position.

Procedural ultrasound has two phases: 1. Pre-procedural assessment: Is there fluid? How much? What is the best site? What structures must be avoided? 2. Real-time guidance: Watch the needle enter the target in real time.

Both phases reduce risk. Pre-procedural assessment alone (static marking) is better than no ultrasound. Real-time guidance is better still, and is standard for central venous access.

2. Central venous access

Clinical rationale

Central venous catheter (CVC) placement under real-time ultrasound guidance reduces arterial puncture, pneumothorax, and number of attempts compared to landmark technique. The NICE guideline (2002) and AHRQ both recommend ultrasound guidance for internal jugular CVC placement as standard of care. Evidence supports its use for subclavian and femoral access as well.

Internal jugular vein

Tip

Video slots: - IJ vein anatomy in transverse — IJ lateral to carotid, compressibility test - Short-axis (out-of-plane) needle guidance — needle tip visualization - Long-axis (in-plane) needle guidance — full needle shaft visualization - Wire confirmation — J-wire in the IJ lumen before dilation

Probe: High-frequency linear (7–15 MHz). Vascular preset.

Anatomy: The IJ vein is anterior and lateral to the carotid artery at the level of the cricoid cartilage. The vein is larger, compressible, and non-pulsatile. The carotid is round, pulsatile, and does not compress.

Approach — short-axis (transverse, out-of-plane): Place the probe in transverse over the IJ vein. Center the vein in the image. Insert the needle at the midpoint of the probe at a 45° angle. The needle appears as a bright dot (hyperechoic) as it enters the image plane. Fan the probe toward the needle tip to track it. Advance into the vein when the tip is visualized in the lumen.

Approach — long-axis (longitudinal, in-plane): Rotate the probe 90° to image the vein in long axis. Insert the needle at the proximal end of the probe, bevel up, at a shallower angle. The entire needle shaft and tip are visible throughout the approach. This approach requires more practice but provides better visualization of needle tip position.

Key steps: 1. Identify the IJ and carotid in transverse — confirm IJ is lateral to carotid. 2. Test compressibility — confirm IJ collapses (not the carotid). 3. Apply color Doppler to confirm venous (non-pulsatile) vs arterial (pulsatile) flow. 4. Guide needle in real-time into the IJ lumen. 5. Confirm venous blood return — dark, non-pulsatile. 6. Advance the J-wire and confirm wire position in the IJ lumen under ultrasound before removing the needle. 7. Confirm wire not in carotid — vein is lateral, compressible, wire appears as a bright line in the lumen.

Teaching points:

  • The most important step is wire confirmation before dilation. Dilating an arterial access is the catastrophic complication. Ultrasound-confirm the wire is in the correct vessel before proceeding.
  • Carotid puncture with a dilator or large-bore catheter requires vascular surgery — do not simply remove the catheter from a carotid.
  • Trendelenburg position distends the IJ (increases venous pressure, enlarges the target).
  • Right IJ is preferred (straight path to the SVC, avoids thoracic duct on the left, lower pneumothorax risk than subclavian).

Subclavian / infraclavicular approach

Tip

Video slot: Infraclavicular approach — axillary/subclavian vein in long axis, in-plane needle guidance.

Probe: Linear, placed in the infraclavicular fossa, parallel to the clavicle, to image the axillary/subclavian vein in long axis.

Approach: In-plane (long-axis) guidance is preferred for the subclavian approach — this allows visualization of the entire needle shaft as it approaches the vein from lateral to medial. The pleura is visible deep to the vein — keeping the needle superficial to the pleura avoids pneumothorax.

Teaching points:

  • The subclavian approach is preferred when a lower infection risk is required (long-term catheters, high VAP risk), or when the IJ is inaccessible.
  • The in-plane long-axis approach dramatically reduces pneumothorax compared to the blind landmark technique.
  • The subclavian vein is not compressible (it is bounded by the clavicle) — use color Doppler to confirm venous flow.

Femoral vein

Tip

Video slot: Femoral vein access — transverse anatomy at the femoral triangle, compressibility, short-axis guidance.

Probe: Linear, transverse at the femoral triangle. The femoral vein is medial to the femoral artery.

Teaching points:

  • Femoral access carries the highest infection rate of the three sites — reserve for emergencies or when other sites are not accessible.
  • Confirm the vein (medial, compressible, non-pulsatile) before advancing. The femoral nerve is lateral to the artery — avoid lateral needle angulation.
  • Femoral access below the inguinal ligament reduces retroperitoneal hemorrhage risk from inadvertent arterial puncture.

3. Thoracentesis

Clinical rationale

Ultrasound-guided thoracentesis reduces the risk of pneumothorax, reduces the rate of dry taps (incorrect site selection), and is now recommended over landmark-based technique for all thoracenteses in the ICU and non-ICU setting.

Pre-procedural assessment

Tip

Video slots: - Effusion assessment — depth, character, loculation - Site marking — selecting the optimal entry point - Safe triangle anatomy

Probe: Curvilinear or phased array, low frequency (2–5 MHz) for depth. Linear for near-field detail.

Pre-procedural checklist: 1. Confirm the effusion is free-flowing — place the patient upright or in lateral decubitus and reassess. A free-flowing effusion layers dependently. A loculated effusion does not shift. 2. Measure the depth — the distance from skin to effusion, and effusion depth at the selected entry site. 3. Identify the diaphragm — confirm the effusion is above the diaphragm (not subphrenic ascites). 4. Look for underlying lung — atelectatic lung floating in the effusion should be identified before needle insertion. 5. Mark the site — apply firm pressure with a marker or a pen cap at the chosen entry point. Mark the depth on the skin.

Safe site selection: - At least one intercostal space below the superior margin of the effusion. - Superior to the lower rib at the chosen interspace — neurovascular bundle runs below the rib. - Lateral enough to avoid the liver (right side) or spleen (left side), but medial to avoid the long thoracic nerve. - Safe triangle: bounded by the anterior border of the latissimus dorsi, the lateral edge of the pectoralis major, and above the 5th intercostal space — the preferred region for lateral decubitus thoracentesis.

Real-time guidance

Tip

Video slot: Real-time thoracentesis — probe in sterile sleeve, needle insertion under direct vision, aspiration confirmed.

Technique: - Sterile probe cover and gel. - Insert needle superior to the rib at the marked interspace, bevel up. - Visualize the needle tip entering the effusion — it appears as a bright hyperechoic dot in the anechoic fluid. - Aspirate — fluid return confirms intrapleural position. - For large-volume drainage, a catheter-over-needle (pigtail catheter) system is preferred.

Post-procedure: - Post-procedure lung ultrasound to assess for pneumothorax (lung sliding should be present). - Re-expansion pulmonary edema risk: limit drainage to 1.5 L per session or stop earlier if the patient develops cough or chest tightness.

Teaching points:

  • The neurovascular bundle runs in the inferior groove of the rib — always enter superior to the rib.
  • In a mechanically ventilated patient, thoracentesis can be performed safely with ultrasound guidance. The pneumothorax rate is not higher than in spontaneously breathing patients when ultrasound is used.
  • If fluid is echogenic (exudate, hemothorax, empyema), a larger-bore chest tube is more appropriate than a needle or small catheter.

4. Paracentesis

Clinical rationale

Ultrasound guidance for paracentesis reduces the rate of failed procedures (dry taps) and reduces the risk of bowel perforation, hematoma, and bladder injury. Real-time guidance is preferred over static site marking.

Pre-procedural assessment

Tip

Video slot: Ascites assessment — free fluid location, depth, bowel loops, epigastric vessels.

Probe: Curvilinear, 2–5 MHz.

Pre-procedural checklist: 1. Confirm free fluid — identify a pocket of ascites without bowel loops or adhesions in the needle path. 2. Measure depth — skin-to-fluid distance and fluid pocket depth. 3. Identify the inferior epigastric vessels — using color Doppler, identify the inferior epigastric artery and vein in the lateral abdominal wall. These run between the rectus abdominis and posterior rectus sheath. Puncture lateral to the rectus sheath avoids these vessels. 4. Identify bowel — confirm no bowel loops in the needle path. Bowel is peristaltic, has wall layers, and may contain echogenic contents. 5. Confirm bladder is empty — especially for low midline approaches. Catheterize if necessary.

Site selection: - Left lower quadrant: Most common site — fewer adhesions than the right (no prior appendectomy site), largest fluid pocket in most patients. 2–3 cm medial and 2–3 cm superior to the left anterior superior iliac spine. - Midline: Infraumbilical, in the avascular linea alba. Useful when lateral ascites is minimal. Avoid if previous midline surgery is present (adhesions risk). - Avoid the right lower quadrant (caecum, appendectomy adhesions) and the flanks (inferior epigastric vessels).

Real-time guidance

Tip

Video slot: Real-time paracentesis — probe in sterile sleeve, needle tip in fluid, “Z-track” technique.

Technique: - Sterile probe cover and gel. - Z-track technique: while inserting the needle, apply traction to the skin laterally or superiorly, then release after needle insertion. This displaces the skin puncture site from the muscle/peritoneal puncture site, reducing the risk of persistent ascites leak. - Visualize the needle tip entering the ascites pocket in real time. - Aspirate to confirm fluid return. - Large-volume paracentesis (>5 L): replace 8 g albumin per liter removed to prevent paracentesis-induced circulatory dysfunction.

Teaching points:

  • Coagulopathy is not a contraindication to ultrasound-guided paracentesis in most patients — the complication rate is low even in patients with elevated INR and low platelets. Routine FFP or platelet transfusion pre-paracentesis is not supported by evidence.
  • Send fluid for cell count, total protein, albumin (to calculate SAAG), culture, and cytology as indicated.
  • Bloody fluid on aspiration: check if it clears (traumatic tap) or remains bloody (hemoperitoneum). A hematocrit on the fluid compared to serum hematocrit helps distinguish.

5. Pericardiocentesis

Clinical rationale

Ultrasound guidance for pericardiocentesis identifies the largest accessible fluid pocket, guides needle direction, confirms needle position in the pericardial space, and reduces the risk of cardiac puncture and pneumothorax. All elective pericardiocenteses should be performed under ultrasound guidance. In extremis, the subxiphoid blind approach remains an option, but ultrasound should be used whenever possible.

Pre-procedural assessment

Tip

Video slot: Pre-procedure pericardiocentesis assessment — largest pocket, optimal window, effusion depth.

Probe: Phased array, cardiac preset.

Pre-procedural checklist: 1. Confirm the effusion — in at least two windows (subcostal, PLAX, apical). 2. Identify the largest, most accessible pocket — the needle must travel the shortest distance to reach the most fluid. 3. Measure the depth — skin-to-pericardium distance at the planned entry site. 4. Identify the path — confirm no liver, lung, or bowel in the needle trajectory. 5. Assess for tamponade physiology — confirms urgency.

Common approaches:

Approach Best For Avoid If
Subxiphoid Large posterior/inferior effusions, emergency Liver in the path, prior abdominal surgery
Apical Large lateral/apical effusions Lung in the path, small effusion
Parasternal Anterior effusions Not preferred (coronary vessels nearby)

Real-time guidance

Tip

Video slot: Real-time pericardiocentesis — needle tip entering the pericardial space, agitated saline confirmation, catheter in the pericardial space.

Technique: - Sterile probe cover and gel. - Insert the needle under continuous real-time visualization. The needle appears as a bright hyperechoic line entering the pericardial fluid. - Agitated saline (bubble study): Once fluid is aspirated, inject 5–10 mL of agitated saline (mixed with a small amount of air). If the bubbles appear in the pericardial space (surrounding the heart), the needle is correctly positioned. If bubbles appear inside a cardiac chamber, the needle has entered the heart — withdraw immediately. - Place a guidewire and dilate over the wire to insert a pigtail drainage catheter for ongoing drainage. - Confirm catheter position with echo — the pigtail tip should be visible in the pericardial space.

Post-procedure: - Repeat echo after drainage to confirm reduction in effusion and resolution of tamponade physiology. - Leave the catheter in place until drainage is <25–50 mL per day for recurrent or malignant effusions. - Send fluid for cell count, LDH, protein, glucose, culture (bacterial, fungal, TB), cytology, and hematocrit.

Teaching points:

  • If the needle hits the heart (epicardial contact), the catheter tip will deflect or “dance” with each heartbeat. Withdraw slightly.
  • Blood aspirated from the pericardial space does not clot (it has been defibrinated by cardiac motion). Blood from a cardiac chamber clots. This helps distinguish pericardial hemorrhage from inadvertent cardiac puncture.
  • In extremis (patient pulseless or prearrest), the blind subxiphoid approach is acceptable — angle the needle toward the left shoulder at 30–45° from horizontal, advance while aspirating.
  • Coagulopathy significantly increases the risk of pericardiocentesis — correct coagulopathy before elective drainage when possible.

6. Integration cases

Cases where procedural ultrasound changes management.

Case 1: Difficult IJ — obesity and short neck

Clinical scenario: A 130 kg man with a BMI of 46 and a short neck requires a central venous catheter for vasopressor administration. The team is debating femoral access due to concern about the neck anatomy.

What you do: Bedside assessment of the IJ with a linear probe before selecting the site.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for assessment.

What you see: Right IJ: large, easily visualized, lateral to the carotid, fully compressible. Depth 2.1 cm. No anomalous anatomy.

How does this change management? The IJ is accessible and low-risk under real-time ultrasound guidance despite the body habitus. Proceed with right IJ CVC using real-time in-plane (long-axis) guidance. Avoid femoral access (higher infection risk) when IJ is accessible. Ultrasound-based assessment has prevented an unnecessary decision to use a higher-risk site based on external appearance alone.

Case 2: Thoracentesis in a ventilated patient

Clinical scenario: A 62-year-old woman on day 7 of mechanical ventilation for ARDS has a large right pleural effusion visible on CXR, contributing to difficult oxygenation. The team wants to drain it but is concerned about pneumothorax risk in a ventilated patient.

What you do: Pre-procedural ultrasound assessment of the right pleural effusion.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for assessment.

What you see: Large, free-flowing right pleural effusion. Maximum effusion depth 7 cm at the PLAPS point. No loculation. Diaphragm clearly identified. Atelectatic lung floating in the effusion. Depth from skin to fluid: 2.3 cm.

How does this change management? Proceed with real-time ultrasound-guided thoracentesis in the lateral decubitus position (if tolerated) or seated position. The pneumothorax rate with ultrasound guidance in ventilated patients is not higher than in spontaneously breathing patients. Mark the entry site, use real-time guidance, and monitor with post-procedure lung ultrasound (restored lung sliding confirms no pneumothorax). This patient’s oxygenation should improve significantly after drainage.

Case 3: Pericardiocentesis — where is the largest pocket?

Clinical scenario: A 58-year-old man with malignant pericardial effusion and tamponade physiology requires urgent pericardiocentesis. The cardiology fellow asks which approach to use.

What you do: Pre-procedural echo to assess all windows and identify the optimal approach.

Tip

Video slot: Loops for assessment.

What you see: Subcostal: large effusion, liver in the subxiphoid needle path. Apical: large effusion anterior and lateral to the apex, 2.5 cm depth from the skin. PLAX: effusion posterior and anterior, 3.0 cm posterior.

How does this change management? The liver obstructs the subxiphoid approach. The apical approach has the shortest path to the largest accessible pocket and no intervening structures in the trajectory. Proceed with apical pericardiocentesis under real-time ultrasound guidance. This pre-procedural assessment has changed the approach and likely prevented a hepatic puncture.

Tip

Procedural principle: Pre-procedural assessment is not optional. The anatomy you assume is not the anatomy that exists.

Note

Q1. You insert a wire for a right IJ CVC. Before dilating, how do you confirm the wire is in the vein and not the carotid artery?

Caution

Answer Confirm wire position under real-time ultrasound. Visualize the wire (bright hyperechoic line) in the IJ lumen on transverse view — the IJ is lateral and compressible, the carotid is medial and pulsatile. If wire location is uncertain, use color Doppler to distinguish venous from arterial flow. Alternatively, transduce the catheter before dilation (venous waveform vs arterial waveform). Never dilate before confirming venous position — dilating a carotid arteriotomy requires vascular surgery.

Q2. During thoracentesis, the patient coughs and the needle contacts the lower rib. What do you do?

Caution

Answer Stop and withdraw the needle — do not advance when the needle has shifted off the intended trajectory. Rib contact risks laceration of the intercostal neurovascular bundle (inferior groove of the rib). Reassess under ultrasound, reconfirm the effusion and the safe window, and re-enter superior to the rib at the chosen interspace. The neurovascular bundle runs in a groove on the inferior aspect of each rib — always enter superior to the lower rib.

Q3. You aspirate blood during pericardiocentesis. How do you determine whether the blood is from the pericardial space or from inadvertent cardiac puncture?

Caution

Answer Two methods: (1) Inject agitated saline — if bubbles appear in the pericardial space (visible as echogenic material surrounding the heart), the needle is correctly positioned; if bubbles appear inside a cardiac chamber, the needle is in the heart. (2) Pericardial blood does not clot (it is defibrinated by cardiac motion), while intracardiac blood clots rapidly. Also: if the catheter tip is visible “dancing” with each heartbeat, it is likely against the myocardium — withdraw slightly.

Q4. Why is the Z-track technique used during paracentesis, and what does it accomplish?

Caution

Answer The Z-track technique creates a non-linear path between the skin puncture and the peritoneal puncture — the skin is retracted laterally or superiorly before needle insertion and released afterward. When released, the skin returns to its original position, creating a tortuous path that resists ascitic fluid from leaking out along the needle track after catheter removal. This reduces the incidence of persistent ascitic leak through the puncture site.

Q5. What is the most common complication of ultrasound-guided central venous access, and how does real-time guidance reduce it?

Caution

Answer Arterial puncture (carotid for IJ access) is the most common and most serious acute complication. Real-time ultrasound guidance reduces arterial puncture by allowing direct visualization of the needle tip entering the venous lumen rather than relying on landmark estimation. The vein is identified, compressibility is confirmed, and the needle is watched in real-time as it enters the lumen — dramatically reducing inadvertent arterial access compared to the landmark technique.
